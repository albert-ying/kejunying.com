General language models are coming for every scientific specialist

Six days ago I argued that future science will run on raw records rather than human summaries. Across 54 raw-record benchmarks and seven frontier models, general language models already beat entry specialists on 38 of 49 tasks and match or beat the median specialist on 37 of 47.

Kejun Albert YingInteractive benchmark · GitHub · Subscribe by RSS

Six days ago I wrote about the future of science without human understanding. The central claim was that scientific concepts, claims and theories are lossy compressions of raw measurements, and that a capable model should eventually reason over the records directly rather than through the summaries people write. That same week, Xinming Tu and Leo Tianlai Chen showed that a frontier language model, handed half a protein sequence with no tools and no fine-tuning, could predict the next amino acid. That result immediately sparked a debate across computational biology: are general language models about to replace protein language models?

I wanted to know whether proteins were an exception or the first visible edge of a much wider shift. Over the past week I built RawSci-Bench, a suite of 54 pure raw-record benchmarks plus two strictly post-cutoff controls spanning six scientific domains and 20 measurement modalities (including seven brain and neuroscience benchmarks spanning resting-state fMRI, T1-CE brain tumor MRI, epilepsy and sleep EEG, motor-imagery EEG and brain snRNA-seq), and evaluated seven frontier language models across the entire matrix with zero tools, zero code execution and zero fine-tuning.

The answer is clear: proteins are not special. Across 24,020 scored answers, general-purpose language models already close 58% to 80% of the median gap to the strongest published state-of-the-art specialist. Measure them against the full published leaderboard on the 49 benchmarks that report multiple domain models, and the picture is sharper still: a zero-shot frontier language model outright beats an entry-level published specialist on 38 of 49 tasks (78%), reaches 88% to 94% of the median published specialist on Claude Fable 5.2 and Gemini 4 Neon, and matches or beats that median specialist on 37 of 47 tasks (79%). Where they still fail is remarkably uniform: dense continuous measurements such as multi-channel EEG, 3D atomic coordinates, low-resolution medical scans and stellar spectra, where plain-text tokenization or generic visual patches fracture the physics before reasoning starts. Yet when a clinical brain MRI arrives at high diagnostic resolution (512 by 512 T1-CE brain tumor slices), all four vision-capable frontier models jump to 90.0% to 92.5% accuracy zero-shot, beating both entry and median published specialists.

You can explore every benchmark, model and control interactively on the RawSci-Bench site.

1. Fifty-four raw scientific benchmarks without tools

Every benchmark in the v2 suite passed two strict filters. First, the input had to be genuine raw scientific data (protein, RNA or DNA sequences, SMILES strings, NMR peak lists, tandem MS/MS spectra, ranked single-cell expression, resting-state brain fMRI functional connectomes, multi-lead ECG or EEG waveforms, clinical tables, or raw clinical MRI, pathology, dermatology, astronomical and satellite images). I dropped ten legacy tasks that leaked metadata headers, wrapped multiple-choice trivia or depended on English prose summaries. Second, every comparator had to be a genuine published domain-trained specialist on the official test split, excluding fine-tuned language models, zero-shot vision-language models and near-chance baselines.

The resulting 54 main benchmarks span six domains: biological sequences (15 tasks), molecules and materials (15), cells and omics (4), physiological signals and images (12, including seven brain and neuroscience benchmarks), health records and society (2), and earth and physical systems (6). Each task samples 40 units from the official public test split with a fixed seed (20 on Stargazer, 32 on MoleculeACE activity cliffs), strips dataset names and sample identifiers, and scores model answers using the benchmark's own evaluation code. Every task is paired against a trivial baseline, the published domain specialist and an identical prompt with the raw record removed.

All seven frontier models finished 100% of their eligible suite: Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.2 Sol and GPT-6.2 Astra completed all 54 main benchmarks plus both post-cutoff controls (56 of 56), while Gemini 4 Neon, Gemini 4 Hydrogen and Gemini 3.8 Flash completed all 45 non-image text benchmarks plus both post-cutoff controls (47 of 47, excluding nine vision tasks unsupported by the text-only Gemini CLI).

Normalize each metric from the trivial baseline (0) to the metric ceiling (1) for skill, and from baseline (0) to the strongest published specialist (1) for relative position. On the 45 shared text benchmarks completed by every frontier model, Claude Fable 5.2 and Gemini 4 Neon lead in a tight finish: Claude Fable 5.2 reaches 0.526 median skill and 0.744 median specialist position (beating 37 of 44 defined baselines and matching or beating the SOTA specialist point estimate on 11 tasks), while Gemini 4 Neon reaches 0.500 median skill and 0.799 median specialist position (0.805 on domain-trained specialists, beating 33 of 44 baselines and matching or beating 8 SOTA point estimates). Claude Opus 5.5 follows at 0.442 median skill (0.645 vs specialist), ahead of Gemini 4 Hydrogen (0.414 / 0.652), GPT-6.2 Astra (0.370 / 0.600), GPT-6.2 Sol (0.351 / 0.575) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (0.333 / 0.486).

Across all 54 tasks including the nine image benchmarks, Claude Fable 5.2 reaches 0.522 median skill (0.709 vs specialist, 42 of 51 above baseline), Claude Opus 5.5 reaches 0.441 (0.631, 36 of 51), GPT-6.2 Astra reaches 0.405 (0.587, 35 of 51) and GPT-6.2 Sol reaches 0.400 (0.581, 35 of 51). Removing the raw record unit by unit significantly lowers accuracy on 95 of 143 paired comparisons across Claude Opus 5.5 (35 of 51), GPT-6.2 Sol (29 of 48) and Gemini 4 Neon (31 of 44), while raising accuracy on zero.

2. Outperforming entry and median published specialists

Comparing a zero-shot language model solely against the single winning entry on each leaderboard hides a practical reality: most scientific labs do not deploy the winning ensemble from last month's competition. They train a standard published domain model such as a GCN, CNN, ResNet, LSTM, ESM-1v, DeepNovo or classical RNA folder. To see where general language models sit inside real scientific leaderboards, I compiled verified entry-level (weakest published domain model, excluding dummy floors) and median published domain specialists on the same official split and metric across 49 of the 54 benchmarks.

The shift is striking. Taking the best of the seven frontier models on each task, a zero-shot language model outright beats the entry-level published specialist on 38 of 49 benchmarks (78%, and matches or beats it within 95% confidence intervals on 40 of 49, or 82%). Against the median published specialist across 47 tasks, it outright beats the point estimate on 21 of 47 (45%) and matches or beats it within 95% confidence intervals on 37 of 47 (79%). Evaluated one model at a time, Claude Fable 5.2 alone beats the entry specialist on 34 of 49 benchmarks (69%, median 1.10x of entry and 0.88x of median specialist), and Gemini 4 Neon beats the entry specialist on 26 of 41 text benchmarks (63%, median 1.12x of entry and 0.94x of median). Even the lighter Gemini 3.8 Flash sits at 1.00x of the entry published specialist across 41 text tasks.

Concrete leaderboard placements show what this looks like in practice:

  • Protein clinical variants (ProteinGym, 1,990 human genes, exact same-unit comparison against PoET 200M with zero MSA): PoET 200M scores 0.899 AUROC on the 40-unit sample (0.920 full split). All seven frontier models are statistically indistinguishable from PoET 200M, led by Claude Fable 5.2 at 0.938 and Gemini 4 Neon at 0.889.
  • NMR structure elucidation (NMRArena, paired 1H and 13C peak lists): Claude Fable 5.2 hits 85.0% top-1 exact structure accuracy and Gemini 4 Neon hits 75.0%, both ahead of NMRPeak at 50.0% on the exact same 40 molecules (54.3% full split).
  • Clinical brain tumor MRI (Cheng et al. 512 by 512 T1-CE MRI, official patient-disjoint Fold 1): All four vision models classify meningioma, glioma and pituitary tumor at 90.0% to 92.5% accuracy (Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.2 Astra both hit 92.5%, Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.2 Sol both hit 90.0%, versus 45.0% with the MRI image removed), beating both the Cheng intensity-histogram SVM entry specialist (87.5%) and the BoW-SVM median specialist (91.3%), with 95% confidence intervals reaching Deepak and Ameer's transfer-learned GoogLeNet SOTA (97.1%).
  • Brain fMRI functional connectome (ABIDE I resting-state fMRI, 116 AAL regions): On autism spectrum disorder classification from functional connectivity alone (zero age, sex or clinical metadata), Gemini 3.8 Flash reaches 0.716 AUROC (ahead of Graphormer at 0.635, BrainGB at 0.697 and BrainGNN at 0.713 on the NeurIPS 2022 BrainNetTF leaderboard), while Claude Opus 5.5 (0.618), Claude Fable 5.2 (0.599) and Gemini 4 Neon (0.595) sit just below entry level.
  • Small-molecule ADMET (TDC): On Lipophilicity logD, Claude Fable 5.2 (0.385 MAE) and Claude Opus 5.5 (0.448) both beat rank-1 specialist MiniMol (0.456), and all seven models beat DeepPurpose CNN (0.743). On Ames mutagenicity, Gemini 4 Neon reaches 0.888 AUROC against ZairaChem (0.871). On cardiac hERG block, all seven models (0.848 to 0.876) beat both Chemprop (0.721) and the median TDC specialist AttrMasking (0.778), matching MapLight+GNN (0.880).
  • RNA secondary structure (ArchiveII): Gemini 4 Neon (0.679 F1) and Claude Fable 5.2 (0.631) outperform Vienna RNAfold (0.577), RNAStructure (0.573), EternaFold (0.599) and CONTRAfold (0.617).
  • Proteomics, chemistry and epidemiology: On Nine-Species yeast de novo peptide sequencing from raw MS/MS m/z peak lists, Gemini 4 Neon decodes 65.0% of peptides exactly (ahead of DeepNovo at 37.0%, PointNovo at 46.0% and Casanovo at 53.0%, and statistically tied with PrimeNovo at 70.0%). On USPTO-MIT forward synthesis, GPT-6.2 Astra and Gemini 4 Hydrogen hit 40 of 40 (1.000) against Chemformer (0.928). On RXNMapper-v2 atom mapping, three models hit 97.5% against 89.1%. On CDC FluSight weekly influenza forecasts, all seven frontier models beat the median competing team (0.760 relative WIS), and Claude Fable 5.2 (0.526) matches the winning ensemble Google_SAI-FluEns (0.555 on the same 40 units).

Only 9 of the 49 benchmarks stay below the entry-level published specialist, and every single one is a dense medical image, multi-channel or intracranial EEG, 12-lead ECG, galaxy image or raw DNA promoter classification task.

3. Intrinsic data reasoning versus pretraining literature

A fair question arises as soon as you see frontier language models beating domain specialists: how much of this comes from intrinsic reasoning inside the raw record, and how much comes from recognizing familiar biological entities or applying textbook rules from pretraining corpora?

Answering that cleanly requires separating two orthogonal axes instead of mixing them into one list: knowledge source (how much pretraining literature or entity knowledge could plausibly help) and input channel (whether the raw signal enters as discrete tokens or as dense continuous waveforms and pixel grids).

Separating those two axes makes both effects unmistakable without hiding the hard tasks.

First, there is a real knowledge-source gradient (0.701 to 0.888 to 1.035 median standing vs SOTA). Pretraining entity and biomedical literature knowledge clearly helps on Group 3 (12 tasks: 3 named-gene scGPT benchmarks, named-variable PhysioNet 2012 ICU records, and 8 well-studied protein tasks including CARE, ProteinShake, CAFA3, Bernett PPI, ProteinGym, PEER GFP and FLIP Meltome), accounting for 7 of the 14 SOTA point-estimate wins. On Group 2 (18 tasks: NMRArena spectroscopy, organic reactions, 5 small-molecule SMILES tasks, 3 crystal CIF tasks, classical ArchiveII ncRNA, CB513 secondary structure, Tsuboyama mini-protein unfolding, ABIDE resting-state brain fMRI functional connectomes, plus FluSight and German COVID which carry real calendar dates and locations), models apply textbook physical, chemical, neuroanatomical or epidemiological rules directly to the raw record, beating 17 of 17 entry specialists and matching or beating 16 of 17 median specialists. Here USPTO-MIT is verified invariant under full RDKit SMILES randomization, whereas possible overlap with public PubChem compounds, Rfam families or historical seasons has not yet been randomized out on TDC, crystals, ArchiveII or surveillance forecasting.

Second, even on Group 1 (24 zero-entity tasks where no named gene, drug, date or classic literature family can answer the item), intrinsic signal reading survives cleanly whenever tokenization preserves the signal. Across all 24 zero-entity tasks combined, the overall rate looks modest (10 of 20 entry, 6 of 19 median) only because 15 of those 24 tasks are dense waveforms and raw images. Look at the 9 sparse zero-entity tasks (anonymous M1 annual series, Nine-Species yeast MS/MS b/y-ion ladders, MassSpecGym, Stargazer, synthetic EternaBench RNA, unlabeled DNA in DeepSTARR, Mouse enhancers and GUE, plus Tu and Chen unstudied microbial proteins where Claude draws on raw sequence pretraining rather than papers): frontier models still beat 6 of 7 entry specialists (86%), match or beat 5 of 6 median specialists (83%), and match or beat 5 of 9 SOTA specialists within 95% CI, backed up by the two strictly post-cutoff controls (NEON water temperature and PDB long-range protein contacts).

Third, Axis 2 isolates where frontier models systematically fail. Every single SOTA point-estimate win (14 of 14) and 34 of 36 entry-specialist wins (94%) occur on sparse, discrete or symbolic inputs. On the 15 dense waveform and raw image tasks, SOTA point wins drop to 0 of 15 and entry wins drop to 4 of 13 (31%). Pretraining literature raises the ceiling on named biomedical tasks, but the wall separating wins from losses is perceptual encoding.

4. Two post-cutoff proofs and five channel ablations

Any strong score on public benchmarks raises a fair objection: memorization. Stripping dataset names does not guarantee a model never saw a sequence or reaction during pretraining. Two strictly post-cutoff controls and five paired channel ablations test that directly.

First, on the NEON aquatic ecological forecasting challenge (40 water-temperature forecast windows across 27 sites measured strictly after every model's training cutoff), persistence scores 2.209 CRPS, a 7-day empirical ensemble scores 2.012 and a 30-day linear drift model scores 2.225. Every frontier model beats all three cleanly: Gemini 4 Neon at 1.013, Claude Opus 5.5 at 1.242 and GPT-6.2 Sol at 1.269. Post-cutoff skill matches earlier 2024 to 2026 windows within 0.03 points.

Second, I collected 40 single-chain proteins deposited in the Protein Data Bank between July and November 2026 and asked models to predict long-range C-beta residue contacts (sequence separation at least 24 residues, 11,017 true contacts, base contact rate 1.56%). A sequence-blind rule gets 2.9% precision. On the real post-cutoff chains, all three models output sparse, high-precision contact maps: Claude Opus 5.5 reaches 39.2% precision (217 of 554 predicted pairs correct), Gemini 4 Neon reaches 31.3% (975 of 3,111, F1 0.1405, top-L/5 precision 31.4%) and GPT-6.2 Sol reaches 25.5% (117 of 458). Shuffling each chain internally while keeping length and amino-acid composition collapses all three to chance (1.84%, 2.73% and 1.89%). Stratifying against every pre-July 2026 PDB structure at 80% sequence identity reveals 27 novel chains and 13 close homologs, and every model scores slightly higher on the 27 novel chains (Gemini 0.153 vs 0.115 F1, Claude 0.049 vs 0.030, GPT 0.041 vs 0.028).

Five paired channel ablations on identical 40-unit samples separate genuine scientific reading from shortcuts:

  1. 1. Randomized SMILES (USPTO-MIT): Using RDKit, I randomized every reactant and reagent atom traversal (doRandom=True) and shuffled component order across all 40 test reactions, preserving 40 of 40 canonical molecular graphs while rewriting 40 of 40 strings. Accuracy barely moved: Claude Opus 5.5 scored 0.950 vs 0.975 canonical (+0.025, no difference), Gemini 4 Neon scored 0.950 vs 0.975 (+0.025, no difference) and GPT-6.2 Sol scored 0.925 vs 0.950 (+0.025, no difference). All three still match or beat Chemformer (0.928) on non-canonical strings, confirming graph-invariant chemistry rather than string lookup.
  2. 2. Marker masking (scGPT hPancreas): Masking all four textbook hormone markers (GCG, INS, PPY and SST) still leaves Claude Opus 5.5 at 0.850, GPT-6.2 Sol at 0.875 and Gemini 4 Neon at 0.925 across 14 cell types, proving multi-gene signature reading.
  3. 3. Image removal (Micro-Bench diagnostic control): Text choices alone beat chance (0.643 and 0.403 vs 0.208), yet adding the raw microscopy image drives a +24.7 to +45.5 point jump (to 0.890 and 0.857).
  4. 4. Coordinate removal (ProteinShake negative control): Stripping every 3D backbone coordinate and leaving only residue identities changes nothing (Claude 0.900 vs 0.900, GPT 0.800 vs 0.825, Gemini 0.875 vs 0.900). The models ignored 3D geometry and read the sequence.
  5. 5. CpG removal (legacy methylation age, dropped from v2): Removing all 200 CpG sites while keeping the brief donor demographic header left Claude and GPT statistically unchanged, while only Gemini 4 Neon extracted significant signal from the CpG values (+0.123). On CARE enzyme classification, Claude and Gemini also explicitly name the exact UniProt accession for 7 of 40 proteins from sequence alone.

Those negative controls matter as much as the wins. A benchmark that cannot tell signal reading apart from identifier recall or header shortcuts cannot tell us what a model actually learned.

5. The dense-signal wall points straight to the adapter

Look across all 54 benchmarks and one clean structural boundary separates wins from losses. Whenever a domain specialist recovers at least 70% skill above baseline, the specialist stays ahead on 30 of 36 tasks, and almost every wide gap shares the same root cause: dense continuous waveforms, low-resolution pixel grids or residue-aligned coordinate fields that plain text or generic vision patches break apart.

On physiological and neural waveforms, frontier models extract clear partial signal yet trail specialists sharply: 12-lead ECG (PTB-XL) reaches 0.865 macro AUROC for Gemini 4 Neon and 0.840 for GPT-6.2 Sol against 0.943 for RA-ASFL; 5-class intracranial and scalp epilepsy EEG (Bonn, 178 raw microvolt samples per second) lifts every frontier model far above the 20% chance floor (GPT-6.2 Astra hits 62.5%, Gemini 4 Neon 60.0%, Claude Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 55.0%, versus 15.0% to 25.0% with the waveform removed) while still trailing 1D-CNN specialists at 89.0% to 94.0%; 22-channel motor-imagery EEG (BCI-IV-2a) stalls at 0.440 against 0.964 for DMB-EDN; Sleep-EDF20 EEG stays near 0.625 against 0.897 for LGFNet; smartphone IMU activity (UCI-HAR) hits 0.700 against 0.980 for Light-PTNet. On per-residue protein secondary structure (CB513 Q3), Opus 5.5 (0.242), GPT-6.2 Sol (0.282) and Gemini 4 Neon (0.400) fall below the 0.457 majority floor, while Claude Fable 5.2 leaps to 0.706 against 0.885 for Ankh Large, showing rapid progress yet still stopping short of a native per-token head.

Medical and scientific imaging reveals a telling resolution split. On high-resolution 512 by 512 clinical brain tumor MRI (Cheng T1-CE), all four vision models hit 90.0% to 92.5% accuracy zero-shot, beating both entry and median specialists. On tiny 28 by 28 medical scans and specialized microscopy or remote-sensing benchmarks, the adapter ceiling returns immediately: PneumoniaMNIST chest X-rays (0.807 GPT-6.2 Sol vs 0.989 sample ResNet-50), BloodMNIST microscopy (0.450 Claude Opus 5.5 vs 1.000), OrganAMNIST abdominal CT (0.375 vs 0.925), PathMNIST (0.840 vs 0.993), ISIC 2018 dermoscopy (0.724 vs 0.895), Galaxy10 morphology (0.650 vs 0.897) and EuroSAT satellite land use (0.550 vs 0.970). Dense physical arrays show the same pattern: Stargazer stellar spectra (5% to 20% pass vs 95% classical solver), Matbench Discovery crystal stability (0.250 to 0.364 F1 vs 0.941 DPA-4.1), JARVIS-DFT formation energy (0.155 to 0.210 eV/atom vs 0.027 kgcnn_coGN) and MoleculeACE activity cliffs (1.275 to 1.392 RMSE vs 0.671 SemiMol).

Meanwhile, the reasoning backbone itself is moving fast. Across the 13 shared v2 tasks completed across five Claude Opus generations (4.5 through 5.5), median skill climbed from 0.093 to 0.492, and Claude Fable 5.2 pushes the full 54-task median to 0.522 while running faster per call (7.0 seconds median latency vs 8.4 seconds for Opus 5.5). Across 14 shared tasks, GPT Sol climbed from 0.217 (5.6) to 0.417 (6.2). Test-time compute also pays off heavily: Gemini 4 Neon spends a median of 5,297.0 output tokens (58.7 seconds per call) compared with 452.0 tokens for Claude Fable 5.2 and 198.5 for GPT-6.2 Sol, driving its breakthroughs on NMR structure elucidation, Nine-Species peptide MS/MS, epilepsy EEG and post-cutoff protein contacts.

6. Every domain specialist with the right adapter

That returns us to the question sweeping computational biology. People are asking whether general language models will absorb protein language models only because amino-acid strings already look like text, so the overlap showed up there first.

What RawSci-Bench shows is that protein sequences were simply the first scientific modality whose native representation passed cleanly through a text tokenizer. Whenever a raw record enters a general backbone intact, whether as human missense variants, randomized SMILES reactions, lipophilicity and toxicity graphs, tandem MS/MS peak lists, ranked single-cell transcriptomes, NMR peak tables, high-resolution clinical brain MRI or ecological time series, frontier language models already carry enough scientific reasoning to beat entry published specialists on 78% of benchmarks and match or beat median published specialists on 79%. Where they still trail is almost always a perceptual input bottleneck: multi-lead waveforms, 3D coordinates, low-resolution scans and dense spectra that generic tokenization shreds before reasoning begins.

We probably do not need fifty isolated foundation models relearning physics, chemistry and biology from scratch. If an unmodified language backbone already closes 58% to 80% of the gap to SOTA specialists across 54 raw-record benchmarks, then placing lightweight modality-native encoders for waveforms, spectra, 3D structures and scientific images in front of a general backbone puts almost every narrow domain model on the same convergence curve. The real question for AI in science is no longer whether general models will replace protein language models. It is which standalone domain models will still make sense once the right adapters arrive.
