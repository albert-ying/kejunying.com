Lab research program

Aging shows itself in whole organisms, in populations and in the lifespan of a species, while most AI for biology works on proteins and cells. We model all six scales below and start much of our work at the larger ones.

Species

We expect the longest-lived species to have the most resilient brains. A bowhead whale can live about two centuries and a naked mole-rat outlives other rodents of its size by decades, so we compare their brains with human brains to find protective molecules and cell states.

Population

We use biobank cohorts and human genetics to ask what shapes how long people live and stay healthy. Epigenome-wide Mendelian randomization identified DNA-methylation sites that are potentially causal for aging-related traits, and whole-exome sequencing showed that centenarians carry an 11 to 22% lower burden of rare loss-of-function mutations than controls. Two open resources let the field compare biomarkers on the same data. Biolearn evaluates 39 aging biomarkers on standardized data from more than 20,000 people, and the Biomarkers of Aging Challenge ran an open competition on blinded methylation and proteomic data from 500 Mass General Brigham Biobank participants.

Individual

HealthFlux is a world model built from 502,166 UK Biobank participants. It treats a person's health as one hidden state that changes between visits and is updated by each new blood test, diagnosis, molecular profile or brain and body MRI. In held-out participants it predicts 1,256 diseases and death over twenty years, and it was tested in four independent US cohorts.

For a single person we also estimate biological age. DamAge and AdaptAge measure damaging and adaptive methylation changes separately, and Ageome estimates the epigenetic age of thousands of molecular pathways at once.

HealthFlux preprint · Causality-Enriched Epigenetic Age Uncouples Damage and Adaptation · Ageome

Tissue

We model aging in tissues and organs from the molecules they release and the images they leave. The work includes transcriptomic hallmarks of ageing and mortality shared across more than 25 tissues in four species, plasma-protein models that estimate the age of individual organs, and organ-resolved imaging read by deep learning.

Cell

We study how aging unfolds inside single cells, where coordinated programs and stochastic drift both leave a mark. The work spans single-cell epigenetic aging, the stem and progenitor cells of the long-lived naked mole-rat, clonal B cells that expand with age and drive lymphoma, and foundation models for single-cell data.

Molecule

Proteo-R1 splits protein design into two steps. A multimodal language model reads sequence, structure and text and names the few residues that govern binding. A diffusion model then generates the rest of the sequence and structure with those residues held fixed.

AI agents that do research

Public repositories hold millions of molecular profiles, and most were analyzed once for the question of the study that produced them. Our agents read these data together with the literature, run the analyses and propose what to test. Each result keeps the data, code and papers it came from.

In ClockBase Agent the agents scored public mouse profiles with aging clocks and nominated interventions predicted to lower biological age. In the preprint, one of them, ouabain, slowed frailty progression and reduced neuroinflammation in aged mice.

Models that simulate biology

A model of a biological system should predict its future state and how it responds to an intervention. HealthFlux follows how a person’s health state changes from clinical and molecular records, and predicting the response to an intervention is the next step. ESM-Life relates protein sequences to lifespan across species.

We also study aging from physical first principles, as the slow loss of the stability that repair maintains, and use that theory to decide what the models should predict.

Brain resilience

Both tracks start with the aging brain. Aging is the largest risk factor for Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, yet some people keep their memory into old age with the pathology in their brains, and we want to know what protects them.

We expect the longest-lived species to have the most resilient brains. A bowhead whale can live about two centuries and a naked mole-rat outlives other rodents of its size by decades, so we compare their brains with human brains to find protective molecules and cell states.

In people we model brain aging from molecular profiles of brain tissue, blood and spinal fluid. We define resilience as how far a person’s brain stays ahead of what their age and pathology predict, and we treat whatever explains that difference as a target.
