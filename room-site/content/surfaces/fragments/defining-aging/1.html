Summary

Fields that study living and non-living systems describe them as aging. Like entropy, aging is anthropomorphic in that it belongs to the system specified by chosen defining properties rather than to a physical object. Biology has not agreed on which change defines organismal aging or on how to recognize rejuvenation or slowed aging in an individual. Here we review definitions of aging and equivalent terms in 415 living and non-living systems. We find that nearly all tie the change of a property to an internal state that changes with time, use or exposure. Adding the direction that separates aging from development and recovery, we define aging as the cumulative drift of a system's internal state towards the boundary of the region of state space in which its defining emergent properties persist. Aging is therefore an emergent property of a complex system, and once the system is chosen, its dynamics decide whether and how fast it ages. When slow variables change much more slowly than fast ones and noise is small, a system can age only if regulation and renewal leave some slow variable unrestored. Of four cases of drift towards a boundary, only weakening regulation both slows recovery from a small challenge and enlarges the response. For organisms, with death as the boundary and the distance from death as reserve, biomarkers must capture the state that sets reserve. Rejuvenation must return the drifted slow variables towards earlier values, and negligible senescence requires that reserve show no species-typical fall after development.
