Six days ago I wrote about the future of science without human understanding. The central claim was that scientific concepts, claims and theories are lossy compressions of raw measurements, and that a capable model should eventually reason over the records directly rather than through the summaries people write. That same week, Xinming Tu and Leo Tianlai Chen showed that a frontier language model, handed half a protein sequence with no tools and no fine-tuning, could predict the next amino acid. That result immediately sparked a debate across computational biology: are general language models about to replace protein language models?

I wanted to know whether proteins were an exception or the first visible edge of a much wider shift. Over the past week I built RawSci-Bench, a suite of 51 pure raw-record benchmarks plus two strictly post-cutoff controls spanning six scientific domains and 18 measurement modalities, and evaluated seven frontier language models across the entire matrix with zero tools, zero code execution and zero fine-tuning.

The answer is clear: proteins are not special. Across 22,980 scored answers, general-purpose language models already close 59% to 81% of the median gap to the strongest published state-of-the-art specialist. Measure them against the full published leaderboard on the 46 benchmarks that report multiple domain models, and the picture is sharper still: a zero-shot frontier language model outright beats an entry-level published specialist on 36 of 46 tasks (78%), reaches 90% to 95% of the median published specialist on Claude Fable 5.2 and Gemini 4 Neon, and matches or beats that median specialist on 35 of 44 tasks (80%). Where they still fail is remarkably uniform: dense continuous measurements such as multi-channel EEG, 3D atomic coordinates, high-resolution medical scans and stellar spectra, where plain-text tokenization or generic visual patches fracture the physics before reasoning starts.

You can explore every benchmark, model and control interactively on the RawSci-Bench site.

1. Fifty-one raw scientific benchmarks without tools

Every benchmark in the v2 suite passed two strict filters. First, the input had to be genuine raw scientific data (protein, RNA or DNA sequences, SMILES strings, NMR peak lists, tandem MS/MS spectra, ranked single-cell expression, multi-lead ECG or EEG waveforms, clinical tables, or raw medical, astronomical and satellite images). I dropped ten legacy tasks that leaked metadata headers, wrapped multiple-choice trivia or depended on English prose summaries. Second, every comparator had to be a genuine published domain-trained specialist on the official test split, excluding fine-tuned language models, zero-shot vision-language models and near-chance baselines.

The resulting 51 main benchmarks span six domains: biological sequences (15 tasks), molecules and materials (15), cells and omics (4), physiological signals and images (9), health records and society (2), and earth and physical systems (6). Each task samples 40 units from the official public test split with a fixed seed (20 on Stargazer, 32 on MoleculeACE activity cliffs), strips dataset names and sample identifiers, and scores model answers using the benchmark's own evaluation code. Every task is paired against a trivial baseline, the published domain specialist and an identical prompt with the raw record removed.

All seven frontier models finished 100% of their eligible suite: Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.2 Sol and GPT-6.2 Astra completed all 51 main benchmarks plus both post-cutoff controls (53 of 53), while Gemini 4 Neon, Gemini 4 Hydrogen and Gemini 3.8 Flash completed all 43 non-image text benchmarks plus both post-cutoff controls (45 of 45, excluding eight vision tasks unsupported by the text-only Gemini CLI).

Normalize each metric from the trivial baseline (0) to the metric ceiling (1) for skill, and from baseline (0) to the strongest published specialist (1) for relative position. On the 43 shared text benchmarks completed by every frontier model, Claude Fable 5.2 and Gemini 4 Neon lead in a tight finish: Claude Fable 5.2 reaches 0.539 median skill and 0.764 median specialist position (beating 36 of 42 defined baselines and matching or beating the SOTA specialist point estimate on 11 tasks), while Gemini 4 Neon reaches 0.521 median skill and 0.810 median specialist position (0.817 on domain-trained specialists, beating 32 of 42 baselines and matching or beating 8 SOTA point estimates). Claude Opus 5.5 follows at 0.450 median skill (0.652 vs specialist), ahead of Gemini 4 Hydrogen (0.425 / 0.656), GPT-6.2 Astra (0.370 / 0.632), GPT-6.2 Sol (0.352 / 0.592) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (0.333 / 0.486).

Across all 51 tasks including the eight image benchmarks, Claude Fable 5.2 reaches 0.526 median skill (0.709 vs specialist, 40 of 48 above baseline), Claude Opus 5.5 reaches 0.442 (0.631, 34 of 48), GPT-6.2 Sol reaches 0.400 (0.588, 33 of 48) and GPT-6.2 Astra reaches 0.399 (0.600, 33 of 48). Removing the raw record unit by unit significantly lowers accuracy on 91 of 135 paired comparisons across Claude Opus 5.5 (33 of 48), GPT-6.2 Sol (28 of 45) and Gemini 4 Neon (30 of 42), while raising accuracy on zero.

2. Outperforming entry and median published specialists

Comparing a zero-shot language model solely against the single winning entry on each leaderboard hides a practical reality: most scientific labs do not deploy the winning ensemble from last month's competition. They train a standard published domain model such as a GCN, CNN, ResNet, LSTM, ESM-1v, DeepNovo or classical RNA folder.
